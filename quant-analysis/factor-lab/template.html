Factor Lab

Every ETF's daily move is part market, part something else. Take the market out and what's left is the factor: momentum, value, a sector, a country. This lab lets you strip the market out of 36 ETFs, watch which factors are working, and see how their correlations shift over short and long lookbacks.

rETF=β × rSPY+residual β (beta) is how many dollars of SPY move with each dollar of the ETF, measured over a trailing window. The residual is the factor return: what the ETF did beyond what its beta to the market predicted.

Beta health check
Inside its own window a residual is uncorrelated with SPY by construction, so that tells you nothing. The test is out of sample: hedge each day with the beta measured through the day before, then see if the leftover still moves with SPY. Persistently positive means the beta is too low or stale. Persistently negative means it is too high.

04 · Short vs long lookbacks

Is the relationship changing, or is it noise?

A correlation always comes from a window of past days. A short window (21 days, about a month) reacts fast but is noisy. A long window (252 days, about a year) is steady but slow to notice change. When the short line leaves the long line and stays away, the relationship is probably shifting. When it snaps back, it was noise. Flat-topped steps are one extreme day (the Nov 6, 2024 election rally, the Apr 9, 2025 tariff pause) entering the window, holding the number up for exactly that many days, then dropping out.

05 · The regime

Is everything moving as one?

Average all the pairwise correlations inside a group and you get one number for how much the group moves together. High means one big trade: stock picking is hard and diversification is thin. Low means dispersion: the pieces are going their own ways. On raw returns this is the classic "correlation regime". On residuals it tells you whether sector or factor bets are crowding into one trade.

Rotation intensity: value vs growth
Value and growth residuals sit near −1 correlation by arithmetic, so their correlation says little. The volatility of the spread between them is the real read: high means money is rotating hard between the two styles. Plotted against SPY's own volatility, same units.

07 · Field notes

What to keep in mind

Why not just subtract SPY?

"MTUM minus SPY" assumes a beta of exactly 1. Low-vol and dividend funds run nearer 0.7, small caps and semis well above 1. The leftover market exposure leaks into every spread, so naive factor spreads all look correlated with each other. Lesson 01 shows the gap.

ETFs are rough proxies

Academic factors like Fama-French are long-short portfolios. These ETFs are long-only, so even after removing beta they carry some sector tilt. MTUM in particular rotates its sector bets as winners change.

The beta window is a choice

A short window tracks beta as it changes but is noisy. A long window is stable but can leave market exposure in the residual after a regime shift. The notebook this is based on uses 63 days. Flip the strip above and watch the numbers move.

One day can own a window

Correlation is driven by the biggest days. A single 5-sigma session can set a 63-day correlation by itself, then vanish 63 days later. When a line jumps up, goes flat, and drops off a cliff, look for the event that started it.

Correlation is not a hedge ratio

Two residuals can be 0.8 correlated with very different volatilities. Correlation says whether two things move together, not by how much. For sizing, use beta or vol-scaled weights.

One market factor only

This strips out SPY and nothing else. For bonds, gold and oil, SPY explains little, so their residual is close to their raw return. A full factor model would add rates, dollar and commodity factors.

How the math runs

Log returns. Beta and alpha from an OLS of each ETF on SPY over the trailing window, ending on the day itself, as in the source notebook. Factor return = return − β × SPY: what a position long the ETF and short β of SPY earned, alpha included. (The source notebook also subtracts the window's alpha; that strips out persistent trends, so it isn't used here.) Correlations are Pearson over the chosen lookback.
